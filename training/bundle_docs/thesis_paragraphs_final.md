## Table 14 -- T14_binary_in_distribution_validation.csv

Table 14 reports the in-distribution validation of the five binary detectors in each of the three training configurations. For every configuration, 15% of the training data was held out with a leakage-safe split that keeps exact duplicate flows on one side, and the scaler was fitted on the remaining rows only; the threshold is 0.5 and the positive class is attack. On the combined configuration (679,999 training and 120,001 validation flows) XGBoost is the strongest detector, with an accuracy of 0.9882, an F1 score of 0.9881, a false-alarm rate of 0.0029 and a ROC-AUC of 0.9962, and the four deep models follow closely at accuracies between 0.9827 and 0.9852. On CSE-CIC-IDS2018 accuracy ranges from 0.9680 to 0.9743, again led by XGBoost, with attack recall between 0.9466 and 0.9550 and benign recall between 0.9883 and 0.9942. On TII-SSRC-23 four models reach at least 0.9949 accuracy, but the LSTM falls to 0.8386 because it misses 9,691 attack flows (attack recall 0.8385). The TII-SSRC-23 validation split contains only 195 benign flows against 60,000 attacks, so its benign recall and false-alarm rate rest on very few samples. These are same-source scores; the external evaluation on TRUSTLab (Table 18) measures how far they transfer. The table is unchanged by the XGBoost tuning, which concerns the multiclass model.

## Table 17a -- T17a_xgboost_configuration_initial_vs_tuned.csv

Table 17a compares the configuration of the initial and the tuned 16-class XGBoost. The tuned configuration was selected with Optuna (TPE sampler, 30 trials) on validation macro F1, using the same 15% leakage-safe validation split as the initial model; the test set was not used during selection. The main change is the number of boosting rounds: instead of a fixed 400, training now stops early with a patience of 50 rounds, which settled at 132 rounds. The tuned model uses deeper trees (max_depth 13 instead of 10), a higher learning rate (0.1291 instead of 0.08), a smaller minimum child weight (3.587 instead of 5), row subsampling of 0.918 and column subsampling of 0.643, and adds regularisation through gamma 1.400 and an L1 penalty of 1.571, with a lighter L2 penalty of 0.712 and 512 histogram bins instead of 256. The class weights remain balanced, with an additional factor of 1.71 on the four hardest classes: DoS, Slowloris, Exploitation and BufferOverflow. The objective, the evaluation metric, the tree method, the 66 input features and the 16 classes are unchanged. Validation macro F1 rose from 0.9206 to 0.9299, and test accuracy and macro F1 rose from 0.9313 / 0.9267 to 0.9351 / 0.9305.

## Table 17b -- T17b_decision_thresholds_initial_vs_tuned.csv and T17b_decision_layer_test_effect.csv

Table 17b reports the decision layer after it was refitted to the tuned model. The layer abstains when the top probability falls below a per-class threshold, chosen on the validation split, or when the flow's Mahalanobis distance marks it as out of distribution. Because tuning changes the model's probabilities, the thresholds were refitted: they fell for Benign (0.825 to 0.2), Exfiltration (0.85 to 0.2) and WebBased (0.525 to 0.2), and rose for Evasion (0.2 to 0.525) and MITM (0.2 to 0.55). The out-of-distribution statistics depend only on the scaler and are unchanged, so out-of-distribution abstention stays at 1.04%. With the tuned model the layer abstains on 1.54% of test flows instead of 1.97%, mainly because low-confidence abstentions fall from 0.93% to 0.50%. On the flows it keeps, accuracy is 0.9374 and macro F1 0.9332, compared with 0.9367 and 0.9320 for the initial model. The abstentions are also better targeted: the error rate among abstained flows drops from 33.68% to 21.60%.

## Table 24 -- T24_multiclass_model_comparison.csv

Table 24 compares the multiclass models on the held-out TRUSTLab test set of 280,063 flows. The tuned XGBoost ranks first with an accuracy of 0.9351, a macro F1 of 0.9305 and a weighted F1 of 0.9354. Its 95% confidence interval for macro F1 (0.9296 to 0.9314) does not overlap that of the initial XGBoost (0.9257 to 0.9276), which is listed for reference. Among the deep models, CNN-BiLSTM is strongest at a macro F1 of 0.9207, followed by CNN1D at 0.9202, MLP at 0.9151 and LSTM at 0.9083. The tuned XGBoost therefore leads the best deep model by 0.0098 in macro F1, a larger margin than the initial XGBoost had.

## Table 24a -- T24a_xgboost_per_class_initial_vs_tuned.csv

Table 24a breaks the tuning result down by class. The largest gain is PortScan, whose recall rises from 0.942 to 0.992 and F1 from 0.967 to 0.992 (+0.025). DoS improves from an F1 of 0.671 to 0.686 (+0.015), through recall rising from 0.560 to 0.587, and Exploitation recall rises from 0.867 to 0.902 (F1 0.784 to 0.790). Benign precision rises from 0.979 to 0.999, lifting its F1 from 0.979 to 0.987. BufferOverflow is unchanged at an F1 of 0.802 and Slowloris moves only from 0.764 to 0.765; for both, recall falls slightly (0.774 to 0.763 and 0.891 to 0.875), so the two confusable pairs remain the model's main weakness. The other classes change by at most 0.003. Across all test flows the tuned model classifies 261,881 flows correctly against 260,816, a net gain of 1,065: it corrects 2,936 flows the initial model got wrong and newly misclassifies 1,871. McNemar's test rejects equal error rates (p = 1.4e-53), and the bootstrap 95% confidence interval of the macro-F1 gain is +0.0033 to +0.0044. The improvement is statistically significant but small.

## Table 27a -- T27a_treeshap_configuration_initial_vs_tuned.csv

Table 27a documents the TreeSHAP configuration and its checks for both models. Attributions were computed with TreeExplainer on the GPU, in log-odds, using tree-path-dependent feature perturbation, for a stratified sample of 8000 test flows with 500 per class and all 66 features. The additivity check, which requires the SHAP values plus the base value to reproduce the model output, passes for both models: the largest error is 1.55e-05 for the initial model and 5.96e-06 for the tuned model, far below the tolerance of 1e-03. In both cases the class with the largest reconstructed output is the predicted class for every sampled flow (1.000). The features shown carry 0.769 of the total absolute attribution for the tuned model, against 0.728 before, so the explanation is slightly more concentrated. Five features are never used by the trees of either model. Because the saved tuned booster holds exactly the 132 rounds it predicts with, TreeExplainer explains the same model that makes the predictions.

## Table 28 -- T28_shap_top6_features_per_class_tuned.csv

Table 28 lists, for each class, the six features with the largest mean absolute SHAP value over the sampled flows whose true class is that class, as computed for the tuned model. Several classes are dominated by a single feature: DNS by Fwd Packet Length Min (5.96), Evasion by Dst Port (4.91), MITM by Bwd Bytes/Bulk Avg (3.93) and API by Bwd Bulk Rate Avg (2.96). DDoS and PortScan share the same leading feature, Bwd Packet Length Max (3.53 and 3.50), and DoS and Slowloris both lead with Fwd Packet Length Max (1.09 and 1.47); this shared evidence is examined against the observed confusion in Table 30. Exploitation has the weakest attribution of all classes, led by Down/Up Ratio at only 0.50, which is consistent with it being one of the hardest classes to separate.

## Table 28b -- T28b_shap_change_initial_vs_tuned.csv

Table 28b shows how the explanation changed with tuning. The leading feature is unchanged for 13 of 16 classes. It changes for C2Beaconing (Bwd Bulk Rate Avg to Bwd PSH Flags), TLSSSL (Bwd Packet Length Max to Bwd Packet Length Std) and WebBased (Fwd Packet Length Std to Fwd Packet Length Max), and in each of these cases the new leading feature was already among the initial model's leading features. Between four and six of the six leading features are shared for every class, and the per-class profiles of mean absolute SHAP correlate at 0.877 to 0.996 between the two models. The tuning therefore changed the detail of the explanations but not their character, and the interpretation built on the initial model carries over to the deployed one.

## Table 30 -- T30_shap_overlap_vs_confusion_tuned.csv

Table 30 relates how similar two classes' attributions are to how often the tuned model confuses them. It lists the eight pairs whose confusion exceeds 1%, where confusion is the larger of the two directions as a share of the true class, plus the most similar pair of all 120 for contrast. DoS and Slowloris are both highly similar (rank 2, correlation 0.897, 8 of the top 10 features shared) and the most confused pair (41.3%), so for them shared evidence and confusion coincide. BufferOverflow and Exploitation, by contrast, are the second most confused pair (23.7%) although their attributions are dissimilar (rank 54, correlation 0.191). And the most similar pair, DDoS and PortScan (rank 1, correlation 0.971, 7 of 10 shared), is almost never confused (0.7%). Similar attribution is therefore neither necessary nor sufficient for confusion. Of the 120 class pairs, 92 are never confused.

## Figure 11 -- fig11_confusion_xgb66_initial.png

Figure 11 shows the row-normalised confusion matrix of the initial 66-feature XGBoost on the TRUSTLab test set, with the share of each true class and the flow count in every cell. Most classes lie almost entirely on the diagonal. The errors concentrate in two pairs: 43.95% of DoS flows (7,043) are classified as Slowloris and 10.94% of Slowloris flows as DoS, and 21.93% of BufferOverflow flows (3,509) are classified as Exploitation and 8.69% of Exploitation flows as BufferOverflow. The remaining notable error is PortScan, where 4.86% of flows (777) are classified as Benign.

## Figure 12 -- fig12_confusion_xgb66_tuned.png

Figure 12 shows the same matrix for the tuned model. The PortScan-to-Benign error falls below 1%, so PortScan now reaches 0.992 on the diagonal. DoS recognition rises to 0.5869 and Exploitation to 0.9018. The two confusable pairs remain: 41.31% of DoS flows (6,619) are still classified as Slowloris, 12.45% of Slowloris flows (1,990) are now classified as DoS, and 23.71% of BufferOverflow flows (3,793) are classified as Exploitation. The tuning improved the classes that were separable from their neighbours but could not separate classes whose flow statistics overlap.

## Figure 13 -- fig13_confusion_change_tuned_minus_initial.png

Figure 13 shows the difference between the two row-normalised matrices in percentage points, tuned minus initial; an increase on the diagonal and a decrease off it are improvements. The largest change is PortScan, whose correct share rises by 5.0 points as the share misread as Benign falls by 4.76 points. Exploitation's correct share rises by 3.44 points, mainly because 2.32 points fewer of its flows are called BufferOverflow, and DoS gains 2.65 points taken entirely from Slowloris. Two classes lose: 1.51 points more Slowloris flows are called DoS, and 1.78 points more BufferOverflow flows are called Exploitation, reducing its correct share by 1.17 points. The tuning thus traded some Slowloris and BufferOverflow recall for larger gains in DoS, Exploitation and PortScan, which is consistent with the flat F1 of Slowloris and BufferOverflow in Table 24a.

## Figure 14 -- fig14_system_architecture.png

Figure 14 presents the complete FORENXAI architecture. The analyst uploads a pcap or pcapng file, whose SHA-256 is computed and recorded when the evidence is acquired (1). CICFlowMeter v4 converts it into flow records with 84 columns (2). Three detectors then run side by side on the same capture: the tuned XGBoost classifies each flow into one of 16 classes from its 66 features (3a); the Tier 1 rules count flow behaviour over 60-second windows for PortScan, DDoS, DoS, Slowloris, Bruteforce, C2Beaconing and Exfiltration, with thresholds in an adjustable rules file (3b); and the Tier 2 rules read the raw capture, with Suricata and the Emerging Threats Open signatures for WebBased, API, Exploitation, BufferOverflow and Evasion and an ARP check for MITM (3c). TreeSHAP explains each prediction (4), and a decision layer abstains when confidence is low or the flow is out of distribution (5). The recommendation stage (6) routes the class through the knowledge map to its NIST SP 800-53 controls and class-specific publication sections, retrieves supporting passages from a prebuilt index, and has the local Qwen model write actions that are kept only if they cite a supplied source that supports them; it reads the prediction, the SHAP drivers and the rule hits, and records whether the rules agree with the model. The AI-generated summary (7) restates the flow's facts sentence by sentence and shows any fact whose sentence fails the check as recorded. The desktop interface and the case report (8) present these results, and the evaluation and integrity layer covers the leakage-safe splits, the validation and test protocol, and the checksums over every file. In the current implementation the rule layer's interface is in place and its output is already consumed by the recommendation stage and the interface; the detection rules themselves are the component still being implemented.
